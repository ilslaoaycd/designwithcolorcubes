Where they came from

The idea behind Color Cubes is older than the toy. In 1704 the French mathematician Sébastien Truchet wrote about square tiles split diagonally into two colors and how many patterns they could make. In the 1830s and 1840s Friedrich Froebel built the first kindergarten around wooden blocks that children arranged into patterns, and his method reached American classrooms in the 1870s.

Color Cubes were made by The Embossing Company of Albany, New York, founded in 1870 with the slogan “Toys That Teach.” It isn’t known exactly when the toy first went on sale, but it was in newspaper toy listings by 1920. Sets came in trays of 16, 36, 49 and 81 cubes, and most surviving boxes date to around 1930.

The toy also had a second life in psychology. In 1914 Francis Maxfield made a set of cubes painted like Color Cubes for a clinic at the University of Pennsylvania. In 1920 Samuel Kohs published his block design test, and his paper says the blocks were the Embossing Company’s, bought at department stores. A block design subtest based on his work is still part of the Wechsler IQ tests today.

Halsam bought the Embossing Company in 1955. Playskool bought Halsam in 1962 and had stopped making Color Cubes by the early 1970s.

Making the replica

The replica started from an original set. Its lid and every loose cube were scanned at 300 dpi, one cube was measured (a 27.5 mm face, the light triangle set inside a 1.7 mm rim with its long side on the diagonal), and the lid’s twelve designs and title were redrawn from the scan. The printed cube follows those measurements in four filaments, and the printed lid is the same artwork, printed face down.

The scans became the cube’s textures, so the original set can be laid out again on screen. These are renders of it, worn paint and all.
