A brief history of Color Cubes

Research notes, November 2026. Also as a Markdown file (9 KB), free to share under CC BY 4.0.

1704: The split square

The basic unit of a Color Cube is a square split diagonally into two colors, and it is much older than the toy. In 1704 the French priest and mathematician Sébastien Truchet published Mémoire sur les combinaisons. In it he described ceramic tiles he had seen that were divided corner to corner into two colored triangles.1, 2 Each tile can be turned to four positions, and Truchet worked out how many patterns could be made by combining them. These are now known as Truchet tiles.1

1837–1870s: Learning through blocks

Friedrich Froebel founded his Play and Activity Institute at Bad Blankenburg, Germany, in 1837, and in 1840 he coined the word kindergarten for it.3 His wooden “gifts” taught young children through building. Gift No. 5 was a cube divided into smaller cubes and triangles, which children arranged into complex patterns.4 Froebel’s method reached the United States in the 1870s, and his block sets came into wide classroom use.5

1870–1920: The Embossing Company

The Embossing Company was founded in Albany, New York, in 1870 by John, Charles and Isaiah Hyatt.6 Its motto was “Toys That Teach.”7 Nobody has yet documented when Color Cubes first went on sale. The earliest known retail listing is a December 1920 newspaper toy catalogue that offers Color Cubes in three different set sizes.8

1914: The cubes enter the laboratory

According to a 1925 article by R. B. W. Hutt, the psychologist Francis N. Maxfield made a set of painted cubes in 1914 for the University of Pennsylvania’s Psychological Laboratory and Clinic. Hutt says they were slightly larger than Embossing Company Color Cubes but colored the same way.9 Maxfield used them to study children’s visual imagery. In his method the examiner built a pattern out of blocks and the child copied it. Hutt adds that the clinic later switched to the commercial cubes.9

Open question: Hutt wrote in 1925 that the Embossing Company’s cubes were “now manufactured.” That leaves it unclear whether the toy already existed in 1914 or whether Maxfield’s cubes came first.

1920: The Kohs Block Design Test

Samuel C. Kohs (1890–1984), a student of Lewis Terman, published “The Block-Design Tests” in the Journal of Experimental Psychology in 1920. It built on earlier work such as Maxfield’s Color Cube Test.10, 11 Kohs’s article says the blocks were made by the Embossing Company and could be bought at large department stores and from Milton Bradley distributors.10 His set was sixteen one-inch cubes with red, white, blue and yellow faces, plus one red-and-white and one blue-and-yellow diagonal face.10, 12 That is exactly the Color Cubes face scheme, and sixteen cubes is the size of the No. 4 box.

Kohs’s main change was to show the target as a flat printed picture instead of a block model.9 His test used 17 design cards of increasing difficulty. It was meant to be easy to give to people with language or hearing disabilities.11 He expanded the work into a book, Intelligence Measurement (Macmillan), in 1923.13

1925: Toy and test side by side

Maxfield published his own description of the method in 1925. He wrote that the blocks were sold as Color Cubes in toy shops and by Milton Bradley and Company, in boxes of sixteen, thirty-six or more.14 The 36-cube box fits the 6×6 sample designs printed on the No. 14 lid.

1930s–present: From toy to IQ test

By the 1930s the Kohs test was in use at the Ohio School for the Deaf and other schools for students with special needs.11 M. L. Hutt revised it for clinical practice in 1932.15 David Wechsler adapted a block design subtest from Kohs’s test for his Wechsler-Bellevue scale.16 The Wechsler-Bellevue appeared in 1939 and later evolved into the WAIS.17 Versions of Kohs’s blocks are still part of the Wechsler intelligence scales for adults and children.18

The toy outlived its maker. Halsam Products bought the Embossing Company in 1955, taking over Color Cubes. Playskool bought Halsam in 1962 and had dropped Color Cubes by the early 1970s.19
